Genomic parentage reveals male breeding dynamics in green turtles under feminization concern

Location: Fernando de Noronha, Brazil • Status: Manuscript in progress • Collaborators: Dr. Mariana Fuentes, Dr. Will White, Dr. Blair Bentley, Dr. Victoria Quennessen, Fundação Projeto Tamar

Overview: Using multi-season SNP-based parentage analysis, I reconstruct breeding structure and mating patterns in a major green turtle rookery to evaluate whether male reproductive dynamics (repeat breeding, polygyny, multiple paternity) could buffer reproduction as hatchling sex ratios become increasingly female-biased under warming.

Why it matters: Climate-driven feminization raises concerns about male scarcity; genomic parentage provides a way to quantify the number and contribution of successful breeders—key inputs for demographic resilience and conservation genetics.

Show project details

My role: Field sampling coordination (female + hatchling sampling), lab processing and SNP genotyping workflow, parentage/pedigree inference, statistical analysis and visualization in R, and manuscript writing.

Methods/toolkit: GTSeq-style SNP panel • parentage reconstruction (e.g., COLONY) • R (tidyverse/ggplot2) • reproducible, version-controlled workflows

Recognition: Archie Carr Award — Best Biology Oral Presentation, International Sea Turtle Symposium (ISTS, 2026).

Study system: Fernando de Noronha (Brazil); genomic processing and analyses at UMass Amherst.

Fieldwork: Nesting beach monitoring and sampling of nesting females and hatchlings to enable multi-year parentage reconstruction.

Lab work: DNA extraction and SNP genotyping (GTSeq-style panel).

Analyses: Estimating sire richness, repeat breeding across seasons, polygyny, multiple paternity, and reproductive skew. Where relevant, I frame sex ratio estimates as a successful breeder ratio (inferred sires relative to sampled nesting females) rather than the true OSR of all sexually active adults.

Outputs:

  • Manuscript in progress (multi-season genomic parentage; mating system + conservation genetics implications)
  • Conference oral presentation (ISTS 2026; Archie Carr Award)

Collaborators & partners: UMass Molecular Ecology & Conservation Lab; FSU Marine Turtle Research, Ecology and Conservation Group; ICMBio/Centro Tamar/ICMBio; Fundação Projeto Tamar; Golfinho Rotador; volunteers and local collaborators in Fernando de Noronha.

What I enjoyed: Nights on the beach and witnessing the full sea turtle life cycle firsthand—from hatchlings emerging and sprinting to the ocean to the same females returning repeatedly throughout the breeding season to nest.

Cold tolerance mechanisms in range-expanding black sea bass populations

Location: Northeastern U.S. coast (range edge vs core) • Status: Ongoing • Collaborators: UMass Amherst • USGS Conte Lab • NOAA Sandy Hook • UConn Avery Point

Overview: I test whether northern, range-expanding black sea bass persist through extreme winter cold via phenotypic plasticity or local adaptation by pairing common-garden acclimation experiments with whole-organism performance assays and tissue-level mechanisms.

Why it matters: Range expansions under ocean warming can still be constrained by cold snaps. Distinguishing plasticity from local adaptation improves forecasts of climate-driven distribution shifts and informs management of emerging fisheries.

Show project details

My role: Experimental design, respirometry and thermal tolerance trials, tissue sampling workflow planning, data analysis (thermal performance curves), and synthesis/writing for dissertation + proposals.

Methods/toolkit: Common-garden acclimation • CTmin assays • intermittent-flow respirometry (RMR/MMR; aerobic scope) • thermal performance curves (Topt, peak, breadth) • tissue biochemistry (Hsp70, oxidative stress, enzymes, lipids)

Study system: Black sea bass (Centropristis striata) from edge vs core populations along the northeastern U.S. coast; controlled acclimation and physiological trials in laboratory settings.

Fieldwork: Collecting fish from multiple populations (range edge vs core) and coordinating logistics for transport and acclimation prior to experiments.

Lab work: Common-garden acclimation at two temperatures (12°C and 18°C); CTmin trials (non-lethal); intermittent-flow respirometry to measure resting metabolic rate (24h) and maximum metabolic rate (post-chase), with individuals tested across multiple temperatures (6–21°C) with recovery between trials. Terminal sampling after performance trials for gill, liver, and white muscle tissues (flash-frozen) plus morphometrics and organ masses for condition indices.

Analyses: Aerobic scope = MMR − RMR across test temperatures; fitting thermal performance curves and extracting Topt, maximum performance, and performance breadth. Mechanism assays include Hsp70 expression, antioxidant capacity and oxidative damage markers (e.g., TAC, MDA), metabolic enzyme activities (citrate synthase; lactate dehydrogenase), lipid quantity and fatty acid composition (membrane unsaturation), and whole-organism condition indices (Fulton’s K, HSI, GSI). I interpret patterns as local adaptation (population differences that persist across acclimation), plasticity (within-population acclimation shifts), or differential plasticity (population × acclimation interactions).

Outputs:

  • UMass Fieldwork Dissertation Grant ($4,725) — supported field collection/logistics for edge vs core sampling
  • Armstrong Graduate Student Research Grant ($22,400) — supported tissue-level mechanism assays (Objective 3)

Collaborators & partners: Lisa Komoroske (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Joshua Lonthair (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Brian Cheng (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Amy Regish (USGS, Eastern Ecological Science Center at the S. O. Conte Research Laboratory); Stephen D. McCormick (University of Massachusetts Amherst; USGS Conte Lab); Dr. Andrij Z. Horodysky (NOAA Sandy Hook); Hannes Baumann (University of Connecticut).

What I enjoyed: Connecting whole-organism performance (CTmin and aerobic scope) to tissue-level mechanisms to build a clear plasticity-vs-local-adaptation story about how range edges persist through winter extremes.
