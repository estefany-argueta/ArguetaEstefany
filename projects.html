Genomic parentage reveals male breeding dynamics in green turtles under feminization concern

Location: Fernando de Noronha, Brazil • Status: Manuscript in progress • Collaborators: Dr. Mariana Fuentes, Dr. Will White, Dr. Blair Bentley, Dr. Victoria Quennessen, Fundação Projeto Tamar

Overview: Using multi-season SNP-based parentage analysis, I reconstruct breeding structure and mating patterns in a major green turtle rookery to evaluate whether male reproductive dynamics (repeat breeding, polygyny, multiple paternity) could buffer reproduction as hatchling sex ratios become increasingly female-biased under warming.

Why it matters: Climate-driven feminization raises concerns about male scarcity; genomic parentage provides a way to quantify the number and contribution of successful breeders—key inputs for demographic resilience and conservation genetics.

Show project details

My role: Field sampling coordination (female + hatchling sampling), lab processing and SNP genotyping workflow, parentage/pedigree inference, statistical analysis and visualization in R, and manuscript writing.

Methods/toolkit: Genotyping-in-Thousands by sequencing (GTSeq) protocol (Adkins et al. in prep)• High Performance Computing (HPC) cluster (Unity) • Parentage reconstruction (e.g., COLONY) • Data analysis using R

Fieldwork: Nesting beach monitoring and sampling of nesting females and hatchlings to enable multi-year parentage reconstruction.

Lab work: DNA extraction and SNP genotyping

Analyses: Estimating sire richness, repeat breeding across seasons, polygyny, multiple paternity, and reproductive skew.

Outputs:

  • Manuscript in progress
  • Conference oral presentation (International Sea Turtle Symposium (ISTS) 2026; Archie Carr Award — Best Biology Oral Presentation)

Collaborators & partners: UMass Molecular Ecology & Conservation Lab; FSU Marine Turtle Research, Ecology and Conservation Group; ICMBio/Centro Tamar/ICMBio; Fundação Projeto Tamar; Golfinho Rotador; volunteers and local collaborators in Fernando de Noronha.

What I enjoyed: Nights on the beach and witnessing the full sea turtle life cycle firsthand—from hatchlings emerging and sprinting to the ocean to the same females returning repeatedly throughout the breeding season to nest.

Cold tolerance mechanisms in range-expanding black sea bass populations

Location: Northeastern U.S. coast (Masschusetts and New Jersey) • Status: Ongoing • Collaborators: UMass Amherst • USGS Conte Lab • NOAA Sandy Hook • UConn Avery Point

Overview: I test whether range-expanding black sea bass persist through extreme winter cold via phenotypic plasticity or local adaptation by pairing common-garden acclimation experiments with whole-organism performance assays and tissue-level mechanisms.

Why it matters: Range expansions under ocean warming can still be constrained by cold snaps. Distinguishing plasticity from local adaptation improves forecasts of climate-driven distribution shifts and informs management of emerging fisheries.

Show project details

My role: Experimental design, respirometry and thermal tolerance trials, tissue sampling and processing, data analysis, and writing for manuscripts and proposals.

Methods/toolkit: Common-garden acclimation • CTmin/max assays • intermittent-flow respirometry (RMR/MMR; aerobic scope) • tissue biochemistry

Fieldwork: Fish collections from multiple populations (range edge vs core)

Lab work: Critical Thermal minimum and maximum trials; intermittent-flow respirometry to measure resting metabolic rate (24h) and maximum metabolic rate (post-chase);. Tissue sampling plus morphometrics and condition indices.

Analyses: I interpret patterns as local adaptation (population differences that persist across acclimation), plasticity (within-population acclimation shifts), or differential plasticity (population × acclimation interactions).

Outputs:

  • UMass Fieldwork Dissertation Grant ($4,725) — supported field collection/logistics for edge vs core sampling
  • Armstrong Graduate Student Research Grant ($22,400) — supported tissue-level mechanism assays (Objective 3)

Collaborators & partners: Lisa Komoroske (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Joshua Lonthair (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Brian Cheng (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Amy Regish (USGS, Eastern Ecological Science Center at the S. O. Conte Research Laboratory); Stephen D. McCormick (University of Massachusetts Amherst; USGS Conte Lab); Dr. Andrij Z. Horodysky (NOAA Sandy Hook); Hannes Baumann (University of Connecticut).

What I enjoyed: I love the hands-on fieldwork, from seining to fishing for our study animals, as well as the less visible work behind the scenes: the long days and late nights spent processing samples, producing data, and turning the collective effort into meaningful results.
